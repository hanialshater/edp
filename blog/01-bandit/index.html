Picking the max when you don't know the numbers

Five things in a row. One is best. You can sample them, noisily, one at a time, and every sample costs you. Pick well, or pay.

Above: three algorithms are pulling slot machines you can't see, racing to accumulate reward. The y-axis is cumulative regret — the gap between what they got and what the perfect oracle would have gotten. Lower curve = better. None of them know which arm is best when they start.

By the time you've finished this paragraph, all three have figured it out. That's the multi-armed bandit problem, and it is the foundation of every interesting question we'll meet in this series.

Act 1 — The classical problem

If I hand you five numbers — 0.3, 0.7, 0.5, 0.4, 0.6 — and ask for the max, you scan, you compare, you return 0.7 with index 1. Done in five steps. It's the most boring algorithm in your textbook.

The interesting version is the one where I don't show you the numbers. I show you five buttons. Each button, when pressed, gives you a noisy sample of a number you can't see. The samples are positive when the underlying number is high, negative when it's low, but they're noisy enough that one pull tells you very little. You have a hundred presses. Or a thousand. Or ten thousand. Pick the order.

Act 2 — The cost of not knowing

This is the situation of every A/B test (which of five email subject lines should I send?), every recommender (which of five widgets should I show?), every slot-machine floor (which of five machines pays best?). The textbook calls it the multi-armed bandit: k arms, each with a hidden reward distribution, one pull per round, noisy reward observed, maximize cumulative reward — equivalently, minimize regret, the gap to the oracle.

You cannot just commit to the best arm after a handful of pulls — early estimates are noisy, you'll often commit to the wrong one. You cannot just keep exploring uniformly — every round spent on a bad arm is regret you don't recover. Every algorithm is a particular way of dividing your pulls between finding out and cashing in.

Act 3 — Three policies

Three policies cover most of the territory. Stupid-sounding ones work surprisingly well; clever ones win by less than you'd think.

  • ε-greedy. Pull the best-so-far 90% of the time; pull a random arm the other 10%. The randomness is your tax on stagnation.
  • UCB1 (Auer et al., 2002). Pull the arm with the highest μ̂ + √(2 ln t / n): optimism in the face of uncertainty. Arms you've pulled little get a bonus that decays as you sample them. Theoretically beautiful.
  • Thompson sampling (Thompson, 1933). Keep a posterior over each arm's mean (a Beta for Bernoulli rewards). Each round, sample once from each posterior; pull the arm whose sample was highest. Bayesian. Embarrassingly good in practice.

Try this: race ε-greedy, UCB, and Thompson at the "hard" scenario (top-two arms very close). Then race ε-greedy and pure-exploit at "balanced". The story tells itself:

  • Pure-exploit commits to the wrong arm about as often as it commits to the right one. It is brittle.
  • ε-greedy beats pure-exploit because the 10% random tax is cheaper than committing to the wrong arm.
  • UCB and Thompson beat ε-greedy because their exploration is directed at the arms you're uncertain about, not uniform.
  • UCB and Thompson beat each other by a noisy margin. Whoever wins will lose next time. Bandits are not where you find your edge.

What you saw, in one sentence

A small amount of cleverness wins by a lot; more cleverness wins by very little. That's the bandit lesson. ε-greedy, UCB, and Thompson are different points on a smooth curve, and any of them is dramatically better than committing too early or never committing at all. Real-world recommenders use Thompson and UCB because the constant-factor differences add up at scale, but the textbook problem is essentially solved.

It's also a very specific kind of problem. k independent arms; one pull per round; reward observed immediately. Most of the things you'd want to do — sort a list, find a path, plan a tour — are bandit problems with structure: arms are not independent, an action consists of several elements, the reward you observe is the sum across them, not the part attributable to each. That structure is where the next four posts go.
