Choosing dinner when you don't know the best restaurant

Five restaurants nearby. One will make you happiest on average. You can try only one tonight, your experience is noisy, and every mediocre dinner costs you a chance at a better one.

That is the multi-armed bandit problem: choose one option, observe one noisy outcome, update your belief, and repeat. The hard part is deciding how many bad dinners you are willing to buy in order to learn which restaurant is actually best.

Tonight's real bandit

You are not optimizing a casino. You are choosing where to eat. Each restaurant is an arm. A visit is a pull. The reward is whether tonight was good enough that you would choose it again. You never observe the restaurant's true average quality; you only observe one noisy dinner at a time.

exploretry a place because you still know little
exploitreturn to the best place so far
regretthe quality you missed by not picking the true best

Act 1 — The classical problem

If I hand you five restaurant scores — 0.3, 0.7, 0.5, 0.4, 0.6 — and ask for the best dinner, you scan, you compare, you return 0.7 with index 1. Done in five steps. It's the most boring algorithm in your textbook.

The interesting version is the one where I don't show you the scores. I show you five restaurants. Each dinner gives you a noisy sample of a quality you can't see. Maybe the chef had a good night, maybe you ordered badly, maybe the place is actually great. One visit tells you something, but not enough. You have a hundred dinners. Or a thousand. Or ten thousand. Pick the order.

Act 2 — The cost of not knowing

This is the situation of every repeated decision under uncertainty: where should we eat, which email subject line should we send, which widget should we show? The textbook calls it the multi-armed bandit: k arms, each with a hidden reward distribution, one pull per round, noisy reward observed, maximize cumulative reward — equivalently, minimize regret, the gap to the oracle.

You cannot just commit to the best arm after a handful of pulls — early estimates are noisy, you'll often commit to the wrong one. You cannot just keep exploring uniformly — every round spent on a bad arm is regret you don't recover. Every algorithm is a particular way of dividing your pulls between finding out and cashing in.

Math object
Unknown restaurant quality:μi = E[Rt | at = i]
Action each night:at ∈ {1,...,k}
Observed feedback:rt ~ Bernoulli(μat)
Cumulative regret:Regret(T) = Σt=1T(μ* - μat), μ* = maxi μi

Intuition: regret is the quality you leave on the table. If Sushi is the true best restaurant and you spend tonight at a weaker place, the difference between those two hidden averages is tonight's expected regret.

The asymmetry: after choosing arm at, you learn only about that arm. The unchosen restaurants do not reveal counterfactual rewards.

Act 3 — Three policies

Three policies cover most of the territory. The simple ones do better than they sound; the clever ones win by less than you'd expect.

  • ε-greedy. Pull the best-so-far 90% of the time; pull a random arm the other 10%. The randomness is your tax on stagnation.
  • UCB1 (Auer et al., 2002). Pull the arm with the highest μ̂ + √(2 ln t / n): optimism in the face of uncertainty. Arms you've pulled little get a bonus that decays as you sample them. It is simple and has strong guarantees, but in short runs it can look conservative.
  • Thompson sampling (Thompson, 1933). Keep a posterior over each arm's mean (a Beta for Bernoulli rewards). Each round, sample once from each posterior; pull the arm whose sample was highest. Bayesian, and consistently strong in practice.

The loop is small. Start every arm with a weak prior. Choose an arm using one of the policies above. Pull it. Update only that arm's estimate. Repeat. What changes between algorithms is not the data they receive, but how they translate estimate plus uncertainty into the next pull.

How the algorithms decide

ε-greedy: simplest exploration tax

Intuition: trust the best average so far, but reserve a fixed fraction of nights for trying something else. This prevents early noise from locking you into the wrong restaurant forever.

μ̂i(t) = si(t) / ni(t)
with probability ε: random restaurant; otherwise: at = argmaxi μ̂i(t)
  • Good: very hard to break; cheap to implement.
  • Bad: keeps wasting ε of traffic even after the winner is obvious.

UCB1: optimism in the face of uncertainty

Intuition: score each restaurant by "what we know" plus "how wrong we might be." Under-sampled arms get a big bonus; the bonus shrinks as evidence accumulates.

scorei(t) = μ̂i(t) + sqrt(2 ln t / ni(t))
at = argmaxi scorei(t)

Where √(2 ln t / n) actually comes from. It is not a magic constant. Hoeffding's inequality bounds how far the true mean can plausibly sit above your estimate:

P[ μi ≥ μ̂i + u ] ≤ e−2 ni u²
pick a shrinking tail t−4: e−2 n u² = t−4 ⇒ u = sqrt(2 ln t / ni)

So the bonus is the highest the mean could plausibly be at a confidence that tightens over time: ln t creeps the bar up slowly as the clock runs, while each pull drops the bonus by 1/√n. Conservative by design — which is why UCB1 can feel statistically slow in short runs.

  • Good: directed exploration and strong regret guarantees.
  • Bad: generic bound can over-explore arms a tighter model would drop earlier.

Thompson sampling: probability matching

Intuition: instead of an explicit bonus, keep a belief distribution for each restaurant. Sample one plausible world; act optimally in that sampled world.

θi ~ Beta(αi, βi), αi = 1+si, βi = 1+fi
at = argmaxi θi

Wide posteriors create exploration automatically: a restaurant with little data sometimes draws a high θi. Known-bad restaurants have narrow low posteriors and almost never win.

  • Good: often excellent empirically; exploration fades naturally.
  • Bad: you need a reward model/posterior that fits the problem.

Contextual LinUCB / LinTS: choose after seeing the situation

Intuition: the best restaurant is not global. It depends on tonight's features: quick, social, spicy, budget. Contextual bandits learn how each arm responds to the context vector.

xt = [1, quick, social, spicy, budget]
r̂a,t = xtT θ̂a
scorea = xtT θ̂a + α sqrt(xtTAa-1xt)
at = argmaxa scorea

LinTS is the same model with the fixed bonus α swapped for a random draw z·√(xtTAa-1xt), z∼N(0,1): optimism versus sampling, exactly as UCB1 versus Thompson above.

  • Good: transfers learning across similar nights.
  • Bad: only works if the context features explain reward.

So is UCB "slow"? Not in CPU terms: basic UCB is just one index per arm. The slowness you feel in the chart is learning slowness. UCB1 uses a Hoeffding-style confidence bound that is deliberately safe for any bounded reward distribution, so it can keep re-checking arms that a more model-aware method would already mostly abandon. In production people often use tighter UCB variants such as KL-UCB or UCB-Tuned, or Thompson sampling when a Bayesian reward model is natural. In fact Bayesian UCB and Thompson are two reads of the same posterior: UCB takes an optimistic high quantile (the upper edge of the belief), Thompson takes a random draw from it. Same belief about each arm; different rule for turning that uncertainty into tonight's pull.

Try this: race ε-greedy, UCB, and Thompson at the "hard" scenario (top-two arms very close). Then race ε-greedy and pure-exploit at "balanced". A few things to watch:

  • Pure-exploit commits to the wrong arm about as often as it commits to the right one. It is brittle.
  • ε-greedy beats pure-exploit because the 10% random tax is cheaper than committing to the wrong arm.
  • UCB and Thompson beat ε-greedy because their exploration is directed at the arms you're uncertain about, not uniform.
  • UCB and Thompson beat each other by a noisy margin; whoever wins one race loses the next. The gap between them is noise.
  • Switch on ε-decay (εt = min(1, 20/t)): it explores hard early, then anneals, so it stops paying ε-greedy's flat 10% tax once the winner is obvious — and catches the directed methods.

Watch the shape, not just the order. random and pure-exploit climb in a roughly straight line — linear regret, a constant cost per round forever. UCB, Thompson, and ε-decay bend over and flatten — logarithmic regret: each new round costs less than the last because the policy is running out of arms it's still unsure about. Fixed-ε greedy sits in between, bending but never fully flattening because that 10% tax never goes away. That bend, from straight to flat, is what a good policy buys you.

Act 4 — Context changes the best arm

The restaurant story has one more real-world wrinkle: the best dinner depends on the night. A quick lunch, a date night, and a rainy solo dinner are different contexts. A plain bandit ignores that and learns one global average per restaurant. A contextual bandit observes features of the situation first, then chooses an arm.

Contextual bandit object
  • Context: before round t, observe features x_t: quick, social, spicy, budget, and a bias term.
  • Action: choose restaurant a_t after seeing x_t.
  • Model: each arm has weights θ_a; predicted reward is x_tᵀ θ_a.
  • LinUCB: choose argmax_a x_tᵀ θ̂_a + α√(x_tᵀ A_a⁻¹x_t).
  • LinTS: the same score with α replaced by a random z∼N(0,1), the contextual Thompson variant.
  • Why it matters: the same restaurant can be bad for lunch and great for a rainy solo dinner.

What you saw, in one sentence

A small amount of cleverness wins by a lot; more cleverness wins by very little. That's the bandit lesson, with one caveat. The jump from "commit too early / never commit" to any principled policy is enormous: it moves you from linear regret to logarithmic regret, a different growth class, not a different constant. Among the principled policies (ε-decay, UCB, Thompson) the differences really are small constant factors — whoever wins the race above will lose the next one. Fixed-ε greedy is the one honest exception: it's principled but still linear, because the tax never decays. Real-world recommenders reach for Thompson and UCB because those constants add up at scale, but the textbook problem is essentially solved.

It's also a very specific kind of problem. k independent arms; one pull per round; reward observed immediately. Contextual bandits add state, but the action is still one arm. Most of the things you'd want to do — sort a list, find a path, plan a tour, compose a page — are algorithms running with missing inputs. That structure is where the next levels go.
