Picking the max when you don't know the numbers

Step 1 of a series on classical problems whose parameters are unknown. The simplest one: max(x₁, …, xₖ). With known numbers it's a one-liner. Without them, you've reinvented the multi-armed bandit.

The classical problem

You have k numbers and you want the largest one. Compare them; return the index of the max. O(k) comparisons, no surprises, done.

The interesting version is the one where you don't get to read the numbers — only sample them, noisily, and pay for every sample. Every casino slot machine is this problem. Every A/B test is this problem. Every time a recommender decides which of k things to show, it is this problem.

The unknown-parameter twist

Reframed: there are k arms, each with a hidden reward distribution. You pull one arm per round and observe a noisy sample of its reward. You want the cumulative reward over T rounds to be as close as possible to T times the best arm's mean. The gap is your regret.

You cannot just pull each arm a few times and then commit to the best — early samples are noisy, you'd often commit to the wrong one. You cannot just keep exploring — every round spent on a bad arm is regret you do not recover. Every policy is a way to spend your pulls along the explore↔exploit axis.

Three policies the textbook gives you, all interesting:

  • ε-greedy. With probability ε pull a random arm; otherwise pull the best-so-far. Dumb and effective.
  • UCB1 (Auer et al. 2002). Pull argmaxi [μ̂i + √(2 ln t / ni)]. Optimism in the face of uncertainty: arms you've pulled little get a confidence bonus.
  • Thompson sampling (Thompson 1933). Keep a posterior over each arm's mean, draw one sample from each, pull the arm whose draw was highest. Bayesian and remarkably hard to beat in practice.

The interactive question

The widget below lets you play this out. Five arms with hidden Bernoulli rewards. You can pull manually, or hand the controls to a policy. Watch the cumulative regret accumulate against the oracle.

The thing worth discovering yourself: at small budgets, the gap between the policies matters more than which "best" policy you pick. At a budget of 100 pulls, ε-greedy ≈ UCB ≈ Thompson ≈ all far ahead of pure exploration or pure exploitation. At 10,000 pulls they converge to nearly the same regret. Bandits are not a hard problem; they are a problem where a small amount of cleverness wins by a lot, and more cleverness wins by very little.

What to take away

If you played long enough you'll have noticed two things. First, every smart policy beats both always exploit (which usually commits to a bad arm) and pure explore (which never commits to a good one). Smart-vs-smart differences are small at large T. Second, regret per pull always shrinks toward zero — given enough pulls, even ε-greedy finds the right arm.

This is the situation a recommender is in when it has nothing else to go on. Every page view is a pull, every click is a noisy reward sample, and the question of "which widget to show" is "which arm to pull." The textbook answer is to use Thompson or UCB and accumulate data; it works, and it is what most production systems do.

It is also the situation we'll spend the rest of the series complicating. Sorting is choose-the-max repeated and made consistent. Shortest path is choosing a max-reward sequence under a constraint. TSP is the same with combinatorial structure that defeats per-arm reasoning. And then EDP is what happens when you stop pretending you know nothing about the arms.

If you have an LLM that knows that fit_reassurance is a good widget for size-anxious shoppers, you do not have to learn it from clicks. You can write the policy and let learning do the small calibrations on top. That is the bet the research project (WPO-Gym / EDP) is making, and Posts 5 and 6 will let you play it against the bandit you just met.
