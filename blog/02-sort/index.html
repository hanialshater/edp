Act 1 — Sort, when you can read the numbers

Mergesort. Quicksort. Heapsort. O(n log n) comparisons against an exact oracle that says "x is larger" or "y is larger" with certainty. The textbook closed this problem in 1962 and nobody has reopened it.

Act 2 — The same problem, but the oracle is unreliable

You have eight wines, eight ad creatives, eight resumes. Pairwise comparisons are noisy: the panel says "A is better" with probability that depends on the true gap. A close pair flips its opinion half the time; a wide pair gets it right almost always. You want the eight sorted, and every comparison costs you.

This is what computer science calls noisy sort or, when the oracle is a Bernoulli with P(i ≻ j) = some function of (μᵢ − μⱼ), the dueling bandit problem. The connection to Post 1 is direct: each of the n(n−1)/2 pairs is its own Bernoulli arm, and you are running them in parallel asking "which side is the success more likely on?" Your budget is the total number of comparisons.

Act 3 — Where to spend the budget

Three strategies in the widget. Uniform Borda queries every pair equally many times and ranks by win count. Mergesort with repeats follows the classical recursion but, at each comparison, repeats it until confident. Active sampling picks the pair whose order is currently most uncertain (its win-rate confidence interval still overlaps 0.5). The active strategy wastes nothing on pairs whose order is already obvious — and on close-to-uniform inputs, that's most of the work.

Active sampling typically beats uniform Borda by a factor of 3–4 in comparison budget at the same τ. Noisy mergesort lands between the two: the merge recursion focuses on the right pairs, but it cannot back out of a comparison whose true gap is too small to ever resolve.

Two solvers, one goal: Bradley-Terry vs the combinatorial bandit

Finding the top-k is one job that two very different machines can do, and putting them side by side is the cleanest way to see what a combinatorial bandit actually is. Both want the best K of N items; they differ in the feedback they consume and the solver they wrap:

  • Bradley-Terry active sorting asks for one pairwise comparison per round, fits latent item utilities by online logistic SGD, and reads the top-k off the sorted utilities. Preference estimation → ranking inference. The natural tool when all you can collect is “A beat B.”
  • Combinatorial-UCB picks a top-k slate directly each round and observes a noisy reward per selected item (semi-bandit feedback). It never models preferences; it optimises the slate online.

This is deliberately not a fair race — Comb-UCB sees K reward samples per round, BT sees a single comparison bit — and that asymmetry is the lesson, not a flaw. The widget replays a 40-run experiment (N=30, K=5, averaged) so you can watch both recover the top-k at the rates their feedback allows.

Both converge to almost the same top-k accuracy by round 1000 — BT 0.90, Comb-UCB 0.91 — but how fast shows the feedback asymmetry. By round 100 Comb-UCB is at 0.80 while BT is at 0.59, and over the full run Comb-UCB pays roughly half the cumulative regret (≈282 vs ≈494). It pulls ahead on speed precisely because it tastes five items every round instead of comparing two. But notice what each produces: BT hands you a full calibrated ranking of all 30 items plus a model of why (the latent utilities), reusable for any k or for downstream preference queries; Comb-UCB hands you a good slate and per-item reward means, and nothing about the items it never selected. Same goal, different artifacts — and the speed gap is the feedback, not the cleverness.

Which is the whole point of the interlude: both are the wrapper. Comb-UCB is optimism + “pick top-k by score.” BT active sorting is uncertainty-seeking pair selection + “fit utilities and sort.” Swap the solver and the feedback channel; keep the loop. The combinatorial bandit is the general online-decision shell; Bradley-Terry is the special case whose base solver is a preference model and whose feedback is a duel.

What you saw

Sorting under noise is choose-the-max in disguise — once for the largest, again for the second-largest, and so on, with comparisons that you can repeat to reduce uncertainty. The budget question is exactly the bandit's explore vs exploit: do you spend comparisons on pairs you're already sure about (cheap signal, no new information), or on pairs whose order you don't know (expensive, but every sample buys real progress)?

Active strategies route the budget to the boundary — pairs whose true gap is small. They win because most pairs in any input have a clear answer and don't need to be queried more than a couple of times.

This is the first hint of structure. In Post 1, every arm was independent. In Post 2, the pairs are coupled by a global ranking: if you know A ≻ B and B ≻ C, you know A ≻ C for free. That free transitive information is what the next post exploits, with a vengeance, when the "arms" become edges of a graph.
