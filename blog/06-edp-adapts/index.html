Act 1 — Why WPO is the capstone

Whole-page optimization contains every problem in the series. The action is combinatorial: choose 6 widgets from 22 and order them. The parameters are hidden: what does this shopper need, and which widgets satisfy it? The feedback is hostile: a purchase or return arrives days later as one page-level number, not a clean per-widget reward.

That last sentence is the production break from the school setting. In the lab, a bandit can observe which edge, pair, or slot earned reward. In WPO production, the learner sees the whole page's result. The semi-bandit decomposition that saved shortest path and TSP is gone.

Production WPO object
  • Action: an ordered slate S = (w₁,...,w₆) chosen from 22 widgets, with no repeats.
  • Hidden reward: true page value R(S,u,c) depends on latent shopper need u and category context c.
  • Observable feedback: delayed scalar R_obs = R(S,u,c)+ε, often days later, with no per-widget credit.
  • Credit problem: if every chosen slot receives the same R_obs, the targets are correlated and attribution is weak.
  • Bayesian-EDP update: fit readable parameters with (R̂-R_obs)^2 + λΣθ((θ-μ_LLM)/σ_LLM)^2.

Act 2 — Bayesian-EDP: prior, plus SGD on the same numbers

The trick is to treat the LLM-authored parameters as a Bayesian prior and run regularised SGD on the same parameters with the observed reward. The regulariser pulls each parameter back toward its LLM-authored mean; the gradient pulls it toward what the data says. With strong regularisation (large λ), you stay near the prior (basically EDP-static). With weak regularisation, you forget the prior entirely and reduce to a per-parameter bandit on the EDP architecture — which the paper calls GreedyLinTS, and which is also competitive but slower to start.

Bayesian-EDP at the right λ is in between: launches at the LLM prior, keeps improving from data, never gives up the readable representation. The widget below races it against the tuned bandit you met in Post 1, equipped with the EDP-derived problem-fingerprint features so the comparison is fair.

Three demonstrated facts, all in EDP's favour at low N:
  1. At 500 sessions, every EDP variant is at ~20% regret while the tuned bandit is at ~30% — a 9.4 pp gap (≈1.5×) from the LLM prior, with zero data.
  2. Bayesian-EDP and tuned LinTS are effectively tied by 10K sessions; the low-N regime is where the prior matters most.
  3. The advantage is structural: EDP-static (which never learns) still beats the tuned bandit for ~2,500 sessions purely on the prior. No exploration schedule recovers it.

Where the series ends

Seven posts ago we were picking which of five hidden options to try, and the bandit literature gave us a clean answer. Then the actions became algorithms: sort, shortest path, TSP. Then the optimizer became an LLM in the OPRO loop. Then the LLM's guess became an executable, editable policy.

The pivot was Post 6: what if you knew something about the problem before the first trial? What if the "arms" were widgets, personas, signals, and constraints — things a human expert could reason about, and now an LLM can too? The series ends here because the research project picks it up: a policy class small enough to be LLM-writable, structured enough to be human-auditable, fast enough to serve in microseconds, and editable as readable code. The bet is that the LLM-edit axis is the unique one — tuned bandits can do regret well — and the empirical evidence is in the paper.

If you've played through the series, you've seen the same lesson from several angles: when parameters are unknown, the algorithm's job is to decide what information is worth paying for. Bandits do this with statistical exploration; combinatorial bandits do it with structural decomposition; OPRO asks an LLM to search from scores; EDP compiles expert reasoning into a readable policy before the first click ever lands.
