Watch what happens for the first ~2,500 sessions: every GAM variant is far ahead. The bandit needs to warm up; the GAM is already competent. Then the bandit catches up. By session 10K, the tuned bandit ties Bayesian-EDP within noise. But the tie is the bandit catching up — never leading.

Act 1 — Recap, in four sentences

Posts 1–4: from-scratch exploration; bandits learn by burning regret. Post 5: an LLM authors a readable policy that's already most of the way to optimal, no clicks needed. The natural worry is that a written policy stops improving the moment the data starts arriving — the world drifts, the prior was a guess, the data has fine-grained calibration the LLM couldn't have known. So the question is whether you can keep learning and keep the readable representation.

Act 2 — Bayesian-EDP: prior, plus SGD on the same numbers

The trick is to treat the LLM-authored parameters as a Bayesian prior and run regularised SGD on the same parameters with the observed reward. The regulariser pulls each parameter back toward its LLM-authored mean; the gradient pulls it toward what the data says. With strong regularisation (large λ), you stay near the prior (basically EDP-static). With weak regularisation, you forget the prior entirely and reduce to a per-parameter bandit on the EDP architecture — which the paper calls GreedyLinTS, and which is also competitive but slower to start.

Bayesian-EDP at the right λ is in between: launches at the LLM prior, keeps improving from data, never gives up the readable representation. Three demonstrated facts, all in EDP's favour at low N:
  1. At 500 sessions, every EDP variant is at ~20% regret while the tuned bandit is at ~30% — a 9.4 pp gap (≈1.5×) from the LLM prior, with zero data.
  2. Bayesian-EDP matches or beats the tuned bandit at every horizon shown; the 10K "tie" is the bandit catching up, never leading.
  3. The advantage is structural: EDP-static (which never learns) still beats the tuned bandit for ~2,500 sessions purely on the prior. No exploration schedule recovers it.

Where the series ends

Six posts ago we were picking which of five slot machines to pull, and the bandit literature gave us a fully-solved problem with very nice convergence guarantees. Five posts of complications later — sort, shortest path, TSP — the same per-arm logic was still mostly working, with progressively more structure that lets the algorithm extract information faster from each trial.

The pivot was Post 5: what if you knew something about the problem before the first trial? What if the "arms" weren't arbitrary numbers but widgets, persons, signals — things a human expert could reason about, and now an LLM can too? The series ends here because the research project picks it up: a policy class small enough to be LLM-writable, structured enough to be human-auditable, fast enough to serve in microseconds, and editable as readable code. The bet is that the LLM-edit axis is the unique one — bandits do regret fine — and the empirical evidence is in the paper.

If you've played through all six widgets, you've seen the same lesson from six angles: when parameters are unknown, the algorithm's job is to find the most informative thing to try next. Bandits do this with random exploration; combinatorial bandits do it with structural decomposition; EDP does it by reading the problem with a competent expert before the first click ever lands.
