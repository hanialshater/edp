SOLVING WITH UNKNOWN PARAMETERS6 POSTS · INTERACTIVE

Solving classical problems when you don't know the numbers

Six interactive posts on what happens to your favourite textbook algorithm when its inputs are hidden — and what changes when you stop pretending you know nothing about the problem.

A side project of the WPO-Gym / EDP research. Each post is a single self-contained HTML file: read, then play. No build step, no accounts, no external libraries. Six widgets, six insights, walking from "I know nothing" (multi-armed bandits) to "I know quite a lot and I keep learning the rest" (the paper's bet).

The series

post 1
Picking the max when you don't know the numbers
Five hidden slot machines, one is best. Race ε-greedy, UCB1, and Thompson sampling on a single chart. Find out why "a small amount of cleverness wins by a lot, more cleverness wins by very little."
post 2
Sorting numbers you cannot read
Eight hidden values, a noisy Bradley-Terry comparison oracle. Race uniform Borda, noisy mergesort, and active sampling; watch the fitted BT skills recover the hidden order live.
post 3
Shortest path when the edge weights are hidden
A 5×5 grid graph. Walk a path, observe its edges, build a per-edge UCB. Watch the per-path bandit fail and the semi-bandit win exponentially — and meet the structural argument behind paper §5.2.
post 4
TSP when the distances are hidden
Eight cities, 2,520 tours, 28 edges. Race random tours against per-edge + 2-opt and watch the action / information gap collapse the combinatorial explosion.
interlude · after post 4
A combinatorial bandit is a wrapper, not an algorithm
The unifying idea: one optimism wrapper, swap the base solver. Watch argmax, Dijkstra, Prim-MST, and 2-opt all learn their hidden parameters through the identical loop — and see where Bradley-Terry and the paper's GreedyLinTS fit in.
post 5
When world knowledge replaces exploration: EDP
A live instance of the actual EDP from the paper — 245 numbers, zero training data. Move persona sliders, watch a page recompose in real time. The point: a competent LLM-authored policy skips most of the exploration you spent four posts learning.
post 6 · finale
EDP that adapts and explores — the paper, playable
Race the LLM-prior GAM against the tuned bandit from Post 1. Real cold-start data from the paper's §5.7. The tuned bandit ties at 10K sessions but never leads — and the regime production lives in is well before 10K.

The arc, in one sentence

Bandits are great when you genuinely know nothing about the problem; they are an expensive way to learn things a domain expert already knows. The series walks from "I know nothing" (Post 1) to "I know quite a lot and I keep learning the rest" (Post 6), and the move that connects them — replacing exploration with authored, editable policy — is what the paper calls EDP.
