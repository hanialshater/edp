Can an LLM explore?

Bandits explore by choosing an action, observing a reward, and updating a posterior. OPRO asks a stranger question: can a language model read only candidate-score history and propose the next thing to try?

Two ways to learn an unknown. Posts 1–4 all learned by exploring from scratch: pull an arm, run a match, drive a route, sample an edge — assume nothing, pay regret, let the data accumulate. That assumption — that you can afford to explore — is the engine of every bandit and combinatorial-bandit on the ladder so far. OPRO trades it for a different engine: reasoning over world knowledge. Show a language model the attempts so far and their scores, and ask it to propose the next candidate. No posterior, no bonus — just a model that already "knows" what a plausible answer looks like.

This is the bridge to EDP. EDP is not just "ask an LLM for a policy." It works because the policy has a representation the LLM can edit — and because it fuses the two engines: the LLM's world-knowledge prior plus the explore-and-update loop from Posts 1–4. OPRO is the simpler half of that fusion, isolated: give the model prior attempts and scores, ask for a better candidate, evaluate it in a black-box environment.

Act 1 — The interface

Every example below exposes the same loop: a hidden objective scores a candidate; the history becomes the prompt; the LLM proposes the next candidate as JSON; the page evaluates it. The LLM is not in the solver path yet. It is imitating an optimizer.

history = [(candidate_1, score_1), ...]
prompt  = "Propose one better candidate as JSON"
next    = llm.complete_json(prompt, history)
score   = hidden_objective(next)
history.append((next, score))
OPRO object (Yang et al., 2023)
Unknown objective:F : 𝒳 → ℝ   (black box — score it, can't differentiate it)
Action at step t:xt ∈ 𝒳   (a candidate string / JSON object)
Feedback:yt = F(xt)   (scalar score; here lower is better)
Prompt state:Ht = {(xi, yi)}i<t, sorted by score
Proposal:xt ~ LLM(· | Ht, instruction)   — the model is the policy π(x | H)

Intuition: a bandit keeps an explicit estimate μ̂ and bonus per arm; OPRO keeps nothing but the text of past attempts and their scores, and asks the LLM to be the optimizer. The model's pattern-matching over Ht stands in for the posterior and the acquisition function at once.

The catch: score-only feedback gives no local gradient ∇F, no calibrated uncertainty, and no validity guarantee — the next xt can be malformed JSON or an infeasible point. This is exactly why Post 6 hands the LLM a structured artifact to edit instead of a free string.

Two engines for the same job

Every post in this series solves one shape of problem: act under an unknown, observe a noisy signal, do better next time. What changes is the engine that turns observations into the next action. The series has shown one engine in four costumes; OPRO is the second engine; EDP is the fusion.

Explore from scratch

Posts 1–4. Assume nothing. Keep a per-element estimate + uncertainty, act optimistically, observe, update.

restaurant arms → player matches → route edges → tour legs

Needs: a budget you can afford to spend exploring. Brings: guarantees, no prior knowledge required.

Reason from world knowledge

This post. Don't model the objective — show the attempts and scores to an LLM and let its prior over "plausible answers" propose the next one.

xt+1 ~ πLLM(x | sorted history)

Needs: a model that knows the domain. Brings: warm starts, native strings/JSON — but no gradient, no calibrated uncertainty.

Fuse the two → EDP

Post 6. Let the LLM author a structured, editable policy from world knowledge, then keep the explore-and-update loop running on its parameters.

action = solver(problem, LLM_prior + SGD_correction)

Needs: a representation closed under both LLM edits and gradient updates. Brings: cold-start and continued learning.

So the question in this post's title — can an LLM explore? — is really "is reasoning-from-knowledge a viable engine on its own?" The widget lets you feel the answer: sometimes a single proposal lands in a great region a from-scratch search would take dozens of samples to find; sometimes the model stalls or proposes nonsense, because unlike the bandits of Posts 1–4 it has no scored model of the objective to correct itself against.

What kind of optimizer is the LLM imitating?

Random / hill-climbing: the dumb baselines

Intuition: perturb the best candidate so far and keep what scores better. No model of F, just trial and error.

xt+1 = x* + ε,  ε ~ 𝒩(0, σ²I);  accept if F(xt+1) < F(x*)
  • Good: trivial, needs no gradient.
  • Bad: blind in high dimensions; wastes the structure in the score history.

Bayesian optimization: a posterior + an acquisition rule

Intuition: fit a surrogate (a Gaussian process) to the observed (xi, yi), then pick the next point by an explicit explore/exploit rule — the same optimism we saw in UCB.

xt+1 = argminx [ μ̂(x) − κ·σ̂(x) ]  (lower confidence bound)

OPRO is the model-free shadow of this: the LLM never writes down μ̂ or σ̂, but a good proposal balances "near the best so far" (exploit) with "somewhere new" (explore) — the acquisition trade-off, done implicitly in text.

  • Good: sample-efficient, calibrated uncertainty.
  • Bad: needs a numeric space + a kernel; awkward for strings, routes, or JSON.

Evolution strategies: a population that drifts toward good regions

Intuition: sample a population, score it, move the sampling distribution toward the winners. Gradient-free, handles odd search spaces.

mt+1 = mt + η Σk wk (xk − mt),  wk ranked by score
  • Good: robust on rugged, non-differentiable landscapes.
  • Bad: many evaluations per step; no semantic prior about what a "reasonable" candidate looks like.

OPRO: the LLM as a learned, semantic proposal distribution

Intuition: the prompt is the algorithm. Feed the sorted history and an instruction; the model proposes the next candidate. It brings a prior no numeric optimizer has — it "knows" what a plausible regression coefficient, tour, or feasible point looks like.

xt+1 ~ πLLM(x | sort(Ht), instruction)

That semantic prior is the upside and the danger: it can leap to a good region in one shot, or confidently propose an invalid or non-improving move because it has no scored model of F to check itself against.

  • Good: handles strings/JSON natively; warm priors; zero tuning.
  • Bad: no gradient or uncertainty; can stall, repeat, or emit malformed output.
The result that motivates EDP (paper §5.4)

In the WPO-Gym ablation, the same LLM and edit grammar were given two prompt diets. Score-only (OPRO-style) feedback is measurably weaker than structured context (domain priors + a diagnostic report):

OPRO prompt:Ht = (edit_batchi, batch_regreti) pairs only
Structured prompt:priors ⊕ per-persona regret ⊕ widget activation ⊕ Ht
LLM simulator:structured 13.3%  vs  OPRO 15.8%  vs  static 19.8% oracle-regret

Reading: on the harder simulator, removing the structure costs ~2.4 pp (≈2.2σ at N=3–4 — suggestive, not conclusive). Score-only search captures some of the gain by probing plausible directions, but the structured artifact is what lets the editor reason about which curve to nudge. That artifact is the subject of the next post.

We actually ran this with LLM agents

The widget above lets you be the optimizer or call your own model. To stress-test the thesis directly, we pointed three general-purpose LLM agents at these exact three objectives and let each run the real OPRO loop: propose a JSON candidate, score it through a black-box CLI (blog/components/opro_score.py), read back only the scalar, reason over the growing (candidate, score) history, repeat. Each agent was blind to the scoring source — it never saw the objective, only its own trajectory of scores.

The pattern is exactly what the title question deserves: yes, an LLM can explore — until the feedback channel runs out. On the smooth regression bowl the agent walked straight downhill; on the tiny TSP it found the global optimum (the best tour was even the first seed, and every 2-opt-style swap it reasoned about confirmed no edge could be improved). But on the constrained LP it felt the penalty walls through score jumps — pushing x past ≈1.6 or y up toward 0.8 made the score leap from −5 into the positives — exploited the corner that gave, and settled at −5.60 instead of the true −5.80. With no gradient and no calibrated uncertainty, it had no way to see that a different binding corner was better. That near-miss is the OPRO fragility made concrete, and the setup for the next post.

Act 2 — What this teaches

OPRO can be surprisingly competent when candidates are short and scores are informative. It can also get stuck, hallucinate invalid JSON, or make locally plausible moves that do not improve the hidden objective. That fragility is the point: score-only search is a weak feedback channel — weaker, often, than the directed exploration a UCB or Thompson agent gets almost for free, because those at least carry a calibrated estimate of what they don't yet know.

So neither engine wins outright. Explore-from-scratch (Posts 1–4) is principled but cold — it must pay for every bit of knowledge in regret. Reason-from-knowledge (OPRO) starts warm but flies blind — no posterior to keep it honest. EDP is the move that takes both: instead of asking the model to optimize arbitrary strings from score history, give it a representation whose fields mean something — curves, weights, signals, slots, constraints. The LLM uses human-like reasoning to author a structured prior (the warm start OPRO gives you), and the Gym environment keeps supplying empirical scores that update those same readable parameters (the explore-and-correct loop the bandits gave you). World knowledge for the cold-start; data for the rest.
