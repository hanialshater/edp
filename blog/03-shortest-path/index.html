Shortest path when the edge weights are hidden

A grid graph. Hidden edge weights. You can walk a path and learn the weights of edges you stepped on. How few walks until you know the best route?

Two algorithms above are walking paths from the top-left corner to the bottom-right of a hidden-weight grid. One treats each path as an arm and remembers cumulative path cost. The other treats each edge as an arm and shares information across paths. The same trial budget; very different regret. The difference is the punchline of this post.

Act 1 — Shortest path, the classical version

Dijkstra. Bellman–Ford. O(V log V + E), give or take. You know the graph; you know every edge weight; you compute the shortest path once and you're done. It's the worked example in every algorithms course.

Act 2 — Now hide the weights

You only learn an edge's (noisy) weight when you walk through it. Each round you commit to a full path; you observe each edge's noisy weight along the way; you pay the sum as your cost. After T rounds you'd like the cumulative cost to be close to T times the cost of the optimal path. The gap is regret.

This setup has a name: online shortest path with semi-bandit feedback. Bandit because you only see the cost of the arm you pulled. Semi because the arm is a *combinatorial* action — a path — and the feedback decomposes per-edge.

Act 3 — Why per-edge beats per-path

Per-path: the number of arms is the number of paths in the graph, exponential in the grid size. After T trials each path has been pulled at most T / |paths| times. With even a modest 4×4 grid, that's hundreds of paths and you'll never sample most of them. Regret is bad.

Per-edge: each edge has its own mean estimate, shared across every path that uses it. After T trials each edge has been pulled many times — typically O(T · path-length / |E|). Combinatorial UCB picks the path with the smallest sum of lower-confidence-bound edge weights, finds the optimum in O(|E| · log T) regret rather than O(|paths| · log T).

The structure — knowledge is per-edge, actions are paths — buys you exponential savings. This is the same principle that fails in the WPO paper (paper.md §5.2): there the only feedback is page-level (the sum), not per-slot, so the semi-bandit decomposition is destroyed. Per-arm bandits can't help when you cannot attribute reward to arms.

The per-path bandit lies between the two, often stuck on a suboptimal path because it doesn't share knowledge across paths.

What you saw

Structure transforms a hopelessly combinatorial problem into an easy one. The action space stays exponential — there are still thousands of paths — but the information space is linear in the number of edges. Algorithms that operate on the information space rather than the action space dominate.

The lesson is structural, and the WPO paper bets on its converse: what if the structure is gone? Whole-page reward is "I clicked / didn't click on this whole page" — not "I clicked because of widget 3." There is no per-edge feedback to share across paths. That is why §5.2 of the paper finds page-level attribution to be ten times more harmful than delay or noise: it removes the semi-bandit decomposition you just saw the algorithm exploit, and per-arm bandits revert to their per-path counterpart's miserable regret.
