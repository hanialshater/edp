Crossing Berlin when the traffic is hidden

You have to drive from the far west of the city to the far east. The map of streets is known. The travel times are not — you only learn how slow a street is by driving down it. How few trips until you find the fastest route?

live · CombUCB-on-edges (blue) vs route-as-an-arm (orange) · cumulative regret

Both drivers above are crossing the real berlin52 map, trip after trip, trying to beat the true fastest route. The blue driver remembers each street's typical time and reuses it across routes. The orange driver remembers only whole routes and their totals. Same hidden traffic, same budget; the regret gap is the punchline of this post.

Act 1 — Shortest path, when the times are known

If you already knew every street's travel time, this would be the most boring chapter in your algorithms textbook. Dijkstra solves it in five lines: keep a frontier of nodes ordered by best-known distance, repeatedly pop the closest, relax its neighbours, stop at the target. O(E + V log V), one shot, provably optimal. You compute the route once and drive it forever.

Dijkstra, the whole thing
  1. dist[src] = 0, everything else ∞; push src.
  2. Pop the unvisited node u with the smallest dist.
  3. For each edge u→v: if dist[u] + w(u,v) < dist[v], update dist[v] and remember u.
  4. Repeat until you pop the target.
  5. Walk the predecessors back to recover the route.

Act 2 — Now hide the travel times

Real traffic is not on the map. You commit to a route, you drive it, and only then do you observe how long each street on that route actually took — a noisy sample per street. You pay the total as your cost. Over many days you want your cumulative time close to the time of the true optimal route. The gap is regret.

This is online shortest path with semi-bandit feedback. Bandit, because you only learn about the route you drove. Semi, because that route is a combinatorial action and the feedback decomposes per street — and a street's time is the same no matter which route used it.

Math object
Shared unknowns:we ∈ ℝ+ for each edge e ∈ E
Action / cost:play path Pt, pay c(Pt) = Σe ∈ Pt we

Intuition: a route is built entirely out of per-edge times. Learn the edges and you have learned every route that crosses them — that shared structure is the whole game.

Semi-bandit feedback:observe { we + noise : e ∈ Pt }  (per-edge)
Full-bandit feedback:observe only c(Pt)  (per-route total)

Semi vs full: semi-bandit hands you a labelled sample for each street you drove; full-bandit hands you one opaque sum and leaves you to guess which street was slow.

Per-edge estimate:ŵe = mean of the ne samples seen on edge e
Optimism (the widget):LCBe = ŵe − β·σ·ŵe·√( 2 ln t / (ne + 1) )
Pick the route:Pt = argminP Σe ∈ P LCBe  (Dijkstra on the LCBs)

Optimism, minimised: under-driven edges get a fat bonus, so their LCB dips below the truth and Dijkstra is tempted to route through them — that is exploration, smuggled into the edge weights. As ne grows the bonus shrinks and LCBe → ŵe.

Cumulative regret:R(T) = Σt=1T ( c(Pt) − c(P*) ),  P* = argminP c(P)

What you pay: regret is the extra travel time stacked up against the true optimal route P*. The two curves in the chart are exactly this sum for the two drivers.

Two ways to credit a path

Credit each street, not the trip

Keep an estimate per edge, drop the optimistic bonus on each one, and let Dijkstra stitch the cheapest path out of the LCBs. Every trip updates many edges at once, and an edge's time is shared by every route that uses it — so one drive teaches all of them.

Pt = argminP Σe ∈ P LCBe,   LCBe = ŵe − β·σ·ŵe·√(2 ln t /(ne+1))
Regret:R(T) = Õ( |E| · √T )  — linear in the number of edges
  • Good: knowledge is shared across routes; the bound scales with |E|, not with how many routes exist.
  • Good: the hard combinatorics stay inside Dijkstra — the bandit only keeps per-edge means honest.
  • Bad: needs per-edge feedback. If production only reports the route total, this credit assignment is structurally unavailable.

Treat each whole route as one arm

Enumerate the candidate routes, run plain UCB1 over them, and observe only each route's total. No per-edge sharing: two routes that overlap on 90% of their streets are still two unrelated arms.

at = argmina ( m̂a − bonusa ),   m̂a = mean total of route a
Regret:R(T) scales with the NUMBER of routes — combinatorially many, ~exponential
  • Good: works with the weakest possible signal — just the scalar total of the route you drove.
  • Bad: every route must be explored on its own; what you learn on one tells you nothing about an overlapping one.
  • Bad: the arm set is the set of paths, which is exponential in the graph — the regret inherits that blow-up.

The structural axis: this is the same split the WPO paper draws in §5.2. CombUCB's per-edge credit is exactly the per-slot attribution a combinatorial bandit relies on; route-as-an-arm — one opaque total per action — is page-level attribution. When the signal collapses to the page total, the per-arm credit these methods need is structurally absent, and the same Õ(|E|√T)-vs-exponential gap reappears as the bandit's loss under whole-page reward.

What you saw

The action space is the same for both drivers — the same routes are available. What differs is the information space. CombUCB learns per edge, and an edge's time is shared by every route that crosses it, so each trip teaches many routes at once. Route-as-an-arm learns per route, throwing that structure away, and pays for it in regret that barely bends.

That is the series' recurring move: a combinatorial bandit is just an optimism wrapper around a classical solver. Here the solver is Dijkstra; the wrapper is "run it on lower-confidence-bound edge costs." The hard combinatorics stay inside Dijkstra; the bandit only has to keep per-edge estimates honest. Linear-in-edges information beats exponential-in-routes guessing.
