When world knowledge replaces exploration

Posts 1–4 watched algorithms learn the problem from scratch. What if, instead, someone read the problem and wrote down a sensible policy? In 2025 that someone can be an LLM, and the policy can be a readable artefact that runs at sub-millisecond serving cost.

Live: an actual policy from the research project · 245 named parameters, authored offline from shopping psychology, zero training data. Drag the sliders — watch a page recompose. No clicks were needed to build this; no clicks are needed to serve it.

The previous posts each fixed the same problem from a different angle: how do you find the best k-tuple, edge, or permutation when you can only sample noisily? The answer was always some form of bandit exploration. This post is the move that the rest of the series sets up: if the problem has domain structure that a human (or LLM) can read, you can often write down a competent policy without exploring at all.

Act 1 — The widget you're about to play with

Below is a live instance of the EDP from the paper: a 2-layer GAM with named piecewise-linear shape functions, on the whole-page-composition problem (choose 6 widgets out of 22 for a fashion product page). The whole policy is 245 numbers. Authored by an LLM, offline, from "size-anxious shoppers need fit reassurance" and similar statements — never trained on any data.

Move the persona sliders to simulate different shoppers; watch the 7 problem scores (Layer 1) recompute; watch the 6-widget page (Layer 2) recompose. This is what the policy does at every page render in production: it reads ~15 signals, evaluates ~300 named scalars in pure code, and returns a page in about 1 ms. No LLM in the serving path.

Notice what just happened. You moved sliders; the policy responded with sensible widget choices for each persona archetype. It had never seen a click. The LLM that authored it just knew things about shopping psychology — size-anxious shoppers benefit from fit reassurance and return policy clarity; comparison shoppers benefit from side-by-side cards; outfit-seekers benefit from complete-the-look modules — and encoded those things as numbers in a typed grammar.

Act 2 — Why this is different from "use a rules engine"

Two reasons. First, the grammar is small and named, so an LLM can hold the entire policy in one prompt, edit a curve, and have the change be both readable (a human sees what changed) and executable (the system serves the new version with no retraining). Try this in your codebase: pick a 10K-line rules engine and ask Claude to "make returns_policy fire harder for the size-anxious." It will struggle. Now ask it to set fit_reassurance.on_rem.F32 = 2.4 in a 9 KB JSON. That works.

Second — and this is the load-bearing move — the small named GAM is a fixed compositional structure, not free-form code. Every page decision factors as base + Σ(on_rem · remaining) + Σ(on_cov · coverage) − slot_decay · slot. The LLM doesn't write the algorithm; it writes the numbers. The algorithm runs in microseconds, deterministically, in pure Python or compiled JavaScript.

Act 3 — What this skips, and what it doesn't

Posts 1–4 needed exploration because the algorithms knew nothing about the problem. EDP needs no exploration for the things the LLM already knows: that fit_reassurance addresses size anxiety, that comparison cards help comparison shoppers, that outfit_completion is irrelevant when the persona is paralyzed by choice. These were learnable from clicks but expensive to learn; they are free if you read them off a shopping-psychology textbook.

What it does not skip: calibration. The LLM said "size_chart at 0.6 means moderate concern" but you may need to nudge it to 0.55. It said "fit_reassurance addresses F3.2 with weight 2.2" but production data might want 2.4. These are small calibrations to the LLM's prior, and they should still come from data.

So you have an LLM-authored, zero-data, sub-millisecond policy that is already most of the way to optimal. The natural next question is whether you can still improve it as data arrives, without giving up the readable representation. That's the research bet, and the next post lets you play it.
