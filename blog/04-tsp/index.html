TSP when the distances are hidden

A courier has 52 stops to make across Berlin, start and end at the depot, and one day to do it in. The map shows where the stops are — but the true driving distance between any two only becomes known once a van actually drives that leg.

live demo · 52 real Berlin stops · random vs 2-opt-on-estimates vs CombUCB, racing toward 7542
Three policies plan tours over the same hidden-distance city. The dashed red line is the known optimum, 7542.

The map is fixed; the distances are not free. Each planned tour is one day of driving — 52 legs observed, noisily, then paid for. There are 52! / 104 ≈ 10⁶⁵ distinct tours, but only 1,326 city-pair distances to learn. The whole post lives in that gap.

Act 1 — TSP when the distances are known

Hand the courier a full distance matrix and the problem is the classic travelling salesman: find the shortest cycle through all 52 stops. It is NP-hard, but metric TSP is one of the friendliest hard problems we have. 2-opt local search — repeatedly uncross two edges that cross — lands within a few percent of optimal in milliseconds. Christofides guarantees a tour at most 1.5× the optimum. For this exact instance (TSPLIB's berlin52) the optimum is known: 7542. With the matrix in hand, the courier is essentially done.

Act 2 — Now hide the distances

The courier does not have the matrix. A leg's real cost — traffic, one-way streets, roadworks — is only revealed, noisily, by driving it. Each day she commits to one full tour, observes the 52 legs along it, and pays the sum. After enough days, can she converge on a near-optimal loop?

Viewed naively this is a bandit with 10⁶⁵ arms, one per tour — utterly hopeless. But the arms are not independent: every tour is built from the same shared pool of edges. Drive Friedrichshain→Mitte once on any tour and you have learned that leg for every tour that uses it. That is semi-bandit feedback, and it turns a factorial search into a per-edge estimation problem.

Math object
  • Unknowns: distances d_{ij} for n(n−1)/2 = 1326 city pairs.
  • Action: a tour π — one of n!/(2n) ≈ 10⁶⁵ permutations.
  • Cost: cost(π) = Σ d_{π_k, π_{k+1}}, including the return to the depot.
  • Feedback: a noisy sample of each edge the tour traverses — shared across all tours that reuse it.
  • Optimism: build a lower-confidence-bound cost per edge (mean − bonus·√(log t / n_e)); under-driven edges look cheap, so the solver wants to probe them.
  • Solver: run 2-opt on the optimistic matrix; the recommended tour is 2-opt on the current means. Score against the known optimum 7542.

2-opt is the perfect base solver here precisely because each swap touches only two edges. Deciding whether a swap helps is a comparison of two edge estimates against two others — exactly the kind of per-component question semi-bandit feedback answers cheaply. A method that had to compare whole 52-stop tours could never keep up.

Find a good tour without knowing the distances

52 Berlin stops, edge distances hidden until driven. One chart races three planners toward the optimum (7542): random tours, 2-opt on running estimates, and CombUCB (2-opt on optimistic lower bounds). Press play, or switch "draw tour" to manual and click stops to build and score your own loop.

Within a hundred or so simulated days the CombUCB planner is hugging the 7542 line — a few percent over optimal — having never been told a single distance up front. Random tours sit far above it, forever. The optimism matters: by treating under-driven legs as cheap, the planner deliberately routes through them, learns them, and folds the knowledge back into one shared belief that every future tour inherits.

What you saw, and the series thread

The factorial action space was a red herring. Once observations decompose into per-edge knowledge, a classical solver carries the load: a combinatorial bandit is just an optimism wrapper around an ordinary algorithm. Here the wrapper feeds optimistic edge costs to 2-opt; the same wrapper sat around Dijkstra in the shortest-path post and around comparisons in the sort post. Change the inputs from "given" to "estimated under a confidence bound," keep the solver, and the textbook algorithm keeps working under uncertainty.
