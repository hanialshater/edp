Three policies above are exploring tours over the same hidden-distance instance. The action space is 8! / 8 / 2 = 2,520 tours; the information space is just 28 edges. The post is once again about exploiting that gap.

Act 1 — TSP with known distances

You know the distance matrix; you want the shortest Hamiltonian cycle. NP-hard in general, but Christofides gives a 1.5-approximation on metric instances, and modern solvers handle thousands of cities exactly. The classical problem is hard but understood.

Act 2 — Now hide the distances

You only learn an edge's noisy distance when you traverse it on a tour. Each round you commit to a full tour, observe each edge along it, pay the sum. After T tours, can you find a near-optimal one? This is a combinatorial bandit, but the action space is now factorial.

Act 3 — Two-opt is the natural bandit-friendly local search

Three strategies in the widget. Random tours — your worst case, regret linear in T. Per-tour bandit — same problem as Post 3's per-path bandit, only worse: there are 2,520 arms. Per-edge estimates + 2-opt — each edge has a mean; pick the tour minimising the sum, but you can't do that exactly (NP-hard), so do a few 2-opt swaps on the current estimates and use the result.

2-opt is uniquely well-suited to the bandit setting because each swap only changes two edges. The swap improves the tour if and only if the two old edge estimates sum to more than the two new ones — a comparison between two pairs of edge means. Algorithms that need to compare entire tours never beat algorithms that need to compare a couple of edges at a time.

The 2-opt-on-estimates run converges to within a few percent of optimal in 50–100 tours, even with the noisy distance observations. Random tours stay at ~30% over optimum forever. The information structure — 28 edges sharing knowledge across 2,520 tours — is everything.

What you saw, and the bigger pattern

Four posts in, the lesson should be sticking. The hard part of "I don't know the parameters" is rarely the not-knowing; it's whether the structure of your problem lets per-action observations turn into per-component knowledge. When it does — semi-bandit feedback, per-edge / per-pair / per-arm decomposition — algorithms with the right structure win by an exponential margin. When it doesn't — only the total reward is observed, attribution is lost — you're stuck with brute-force exploration over a combinatorial action space.

So far you've watched algorithms learn the parameters from scratch. The remaining two posts ask a different question: what if you already knew something useful about the problem before the first trial? Not the answers, but the structure — that some widgets serve some customer types, that some routes are obviously bad. Could you skip most of the exploration entirely?

Next: what if instead of running 2-opt for a hundred trials, a domain expert just wrote down a sensible policy? That expert can now be an LLM. Post 5 — when world knowledge replaces exploration.
